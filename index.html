How to rate

A loop is a closed walk: follow the drawn lines from number to number and end up back where you started. The list leaves out the repeat at the end — “0 – 1 – 2 – 3” means 0→1→2→3→back to 0 — and that return is the only thing it leaves out. Every number you step on is written down, and every step, including the last one home, has to sit on a line that is really drawn.

1. The basic idea

A. 0 – 1 – 2 – 3 ✓ traceable: every step follows a drawn line, and 3 connects back to 0.

B. 0 – 1 – 3 ✕ not a loop here: the dashed jump 1→3 has no line in the drawing.

2. Ring drawings work exactly the same way

A. 5 – 0 – 1 – 2 ✓ traceable: 5→0→1→2, and 2 connects back to 5 along the drawn line.

B. 0 – 1 – 3 ✕ not a loop here: no line connects 1 to 3. Sitting near each other on the circle means nothing — only a drawn line counts.

3. Watch out: the walk that never gets home

A. 0 – 1 – 2 – 3 ✓ traceable: closes back to 0 along the square.

B. 0 – 1 – 4 – 5 ✕ not a loop: every step here is a real line, so it reads fine right up to the end — but 5 has nothing going back to 0. It is a dead end hanging off the square. This is the most common trap: the walk is real, the way home is not.

4. Watch out: a stop left out on the way home

A. 0 – 1 – 2 – 3 – 4 ✓ traceable: all the way round, and 4 has a line straight back to 0.

B. 0 – 1 – 2 – 3 ✕ not a loop: it reads like the same trip, and there is a way back from 3 to 0 — but only by walking through 4 (circled), and 4 is not in the list. A node you step on has to be named. The return to the start is the only step a list may leave out, never a stop in the middle.

Expect this one a lot: the two options often differ by a single number. If the shorter list needs an unnamed node to get home, it is the wrong one.

5. Watch out: crossing lines are not junctions

A. 0 – 1 – 2 ✓ traceable: 0→1 and 1→2 around the rim, then one straight line carries 2 back to 0. It passes over another line on the way (circled) — passing over is fine.

B. 0 – 4 – 5 ✕ not a loop: nothing joins 0 to 4. The only way across is to hop onto a different line at the circled spot — and a crossing is just two lines passing over each other, never a corner you can turn at.

The quick way to check

1. Check the way home first. Take the last number and the first one — is there one line joining them directly? The two options usually share almost all their numbers, so this closing step is where they differ, and it often settles the answer on its own. Needing to pass through some other node to get home means no.

2. Then walk the rest. Number, line, next number. Each pair of circles needs one unbroken line between them. The moment a step has no line, that option is out.

3. Ignore the noise. Lines crossing on the way do not count, and two numbers sitting side by side on the circle are not joined unless a line says so.

One of the two is always traceable. If both look wrong, go back and re-check each one’s closing step — that is nearly always where the difference hides.
